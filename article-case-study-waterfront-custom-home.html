Case Study: Coordinating MEP for a Waterfront Custom Home (Illustrative Example)

Note: the project described below is a composite, illustrative example built from patterns we see repeatedly on Florida waterfront builds. It does not represent a specific client, address, or real set of drawings — it's meant to show how MEP coordination actually plays out on a project like this.

Picture a three-story custom home on a canal lot in Southwest Florida: open-concept living space, a wall of impact glass facing the water, an outdoor kitchen and summer kitchen on the lanai, a pool and spa, and an elevator shaft connecting all three levels. On paper, the architectural design is finished and the client is eager to break ground. In practice, this is exactly the point where MEP engineering either saves the schedule or quietly derails it — because the systems that make the home livable have to fit inside a structure that was drawn around aesthetics first.

The first conflict usually shows up in the ceiling. A home with this much glass and this little opaque wall space leaves very few places to route ductwork, so the mechanical engineer has to work backward from the architectural reflected ceiling plan rather than the other way around. On a project like this, that often means splitting the HVAC system into multiple zones — one for the open living and kitchen area, separate zones for each level — and running trunk lines through furred-down soffits that have to be coordinated with the architect before framing, not after. Waiting until framing is underway to discover the ducts don't fit is one of the most common and most expensive mistakes we see on waterfront builds.

The electrical scope tells a similar story. An outdoor kitchen, pool equipment pad, elevator, EV charger rough-in, and a home automation package all draw from the same panel, and on a coastal lot that panel and its disconnects also need to account for flood-zone elevation requirements. Getting the load calculations right up front — rather than guessing at service size and upgrading later — is what keeps the electrical plan from becoming a bottleneck when the utility company reviews the service application.

Plumbing adds its own layer of coordination. A pool bath, an outdoor shower, a summer kitchen sink, and fixtures spread across three floors all need to tie back to a main line sized correctly from the start, and on a canal lot the engineer also has to account for the elevation of the sewer connection relative to the finished floor. None of this is unusual for a Florida waterfront home — but each piece has to be resolved on paper, in a way that satisfies both the Florida Building Code and the local utility, before a permit reviewer ever sees the set.

This is where architectural drafting and MEP engineering have to move together rather than in sequence. In a project like this, the MEP engineer typically flags soffit depth, panel location, and riser paths back to the architect in the first draft round — not after the architectural plans are "final" — so that corrections get made as line-weight changes on a drawing instead of as rework on site. That back-and-forth, done early and quickly, is usually the single biggest factor in whether a permit set like this gets approved on the first submittal or bounces back with comments.

The takeaway for anyone building something similar: the systems behind the walls aren't an afterthought to a waterfront custom home's design, they're a constraint the design has to be built around from day one. A coordinated, permit-ready MEP package doesn't just get a project through the building department faster — it's what keeps the home that gets built matching the home that was drawn.
