Pool and Spa MEP: What Your Plans Need to Include for Permits in Florida

Florida has more residential swimming pools per capita than any other state, which means the state's building departments have highly developed — and sometimes highly specific — requirements for pool permits. If you're planning a new pool or spa addition, understanding what the permit package needs to include will save you from the most common cause of project delays: incomplete or non-compliant drawings. A pool is a significant electrical, plumbing, and structural system, and it needs to be engineered and documented as such.

Electrical Requirements: Bonding, GFCI, and Setbacks

Electrical safety around pools is one of the most tightly regulated areas in the NEC, and Florida enforces these requirements strictly. Every pool requires an equipotential bonding system — a network of copper conductors that connects the pool shell, water, equipment, and all metallic components to a common ground potential. This bonding prevents dangerous voltage gradients in and around the water that can cause electric shock drowning (ESD), a real and preventable hazard.

All receptacles within 20 feet of a pool must be GFCI-protected. Luminaires (lights) installed in the pool must be low-voltage or specifically listed for underwater use and located at least 5 feet from the pool edge unless protected by a GFCI device. No overhead conductors — power lines — may be within 22.5 feet of the pool water surface. Your electrical drawings need to show all of this explicitly, with dimensions, for the plan reviewer.

Plumbing: Circulation, Filtration, and Heaters

The plumbing plan for a pool needs to show the circulation system — including skimmers, main drains (with anti-entrapment covers per the Virginia Graeme Baker Act), return jets, filter, pump, and heater — along with pipe sizes and materials. Florida code requires dual main drains spaced at least 3 feet apart, or a single main drain with an approved safety vacuum release system (SVRS), to prevent suction entrapment.

Pool heaters — gas, heat pump, or solar — require their own permit considerations. Gas pool heaters need a dedicated gas line sized for the heater's BTU input, typically 200,000–400,000 BTU/hr for a residential pool. The gas line sizing calculation needs to be on the drawings. Heat pump pool heaters require electrical service — typically 240V at 40–60 amps — that must be shown on the electrical plan. Solar heaters require roof penetrations and structural review if the panels are roof-mounted.

What a Complete Pool Permit Package Looks Like

A complete pool permit package in Florida typically includes: a site plan showing the pool location with setbacks from property lines, easements, and the house; a pool structural plan (shell design, coping, decking) prepared by a structural engineer; an electrical plan showing bonding, grounding, equipment, and GFCI protection; a plumbing plan showing the circulation system; and a fence/barrier plan showing that the pool is enclosed per Florida Statute 515, which requires a barrier at least 4 feet high around any pool accessible to children under 6.

The barrier requirement is often overlooked or misunderstood. Florida law requires that the barrier prevent direct access to the pool from the house — meaning a door from the house into the pool area must be self-closing and self-latching. A pool that passes electrical and plumbing inspection but fails the barrier requirement will not get a certificate of completion.

Common Pool Plan Review Failures

The most frequent reasons pool permit applications are rejected in Florida include: missing bonding conductor routing on the electrical plan, main drain configuration that doesn't comply with anti-entrapment requirements, insufficient setbacks from property lines (typically 5 feet in most Florida counties, but this varies), missing gas line sizing calculation, and a barrier plan that doesn't satisfy Florida Statute 515. Knowing these common failures in advance — and addressing them in your initial submission — is the fastest path to a permit in hand.
