Duct Design Done Right: HVAC Layouts That Actually Work in Florida

In Florida, your HVAC system is working harder than almost anywhere else in the country — cooling for 10 or more months per year, fighting humidity that can push indoor dew points into the uncomfortable range even when the temperature feels tolerable. The equipment gets most of the attention when homeowners shop for a new system, but the ductwork is equally important. A perfectly sized, efficient air handler delivering air through poorly designed ducts will perform badly. You'll have hot and cold rooms, high utility bills, humidity problems, and a system that runs constantly without ever quite getting comfortable.

Why Duct Design Is Different in Florida

The fundamental challenge with ductwork in Florida is that most ducts run through unconditioned attic space — and that attic space reaches 140–160°F in summer. Every foot of duct in that attic is a heat exchanger working against you, warming the supply air before it reaches the rooms it's supposed to cool. Florida energy code requires duct insulation of at least R-8 in unconditioned attic spaces, but even well-insulated ducts lose efficiency when they're too long or too small for the airflow they're asked to carry.

The second major factor is humidity control. An HVAC system removes moisture from the air by cooling it below the dew point, causing water to condense on the evaporator coil. A system that short-cycles — runs for a few minutes, shuts off, runs again — never fully cools the coil and never removes much moisture. Duct design directly affects whether the system runs long enough to dehumidify: undersized ducts restrict airflow and cause short-cycling; oversized ducts deliver too much air too quickly for the system to remove humidity.

What a Manual D Duct Design Includes

ACCA Manual D is the industry standard for residential duct design, and Florida code requires that duct systems be designed in accordance with it. A Manual D design starts with the Manual J load calculation results and sizes each duct branch to deliver the correct amount of air to each room. The design specifies duct diameters, lengths, number of elbows and fittings, and static pressure throughout the system.

The output of a Manual D calculation tells you whether the system can deliver the required airflow to every room with the available static pressure — the "push" the air handler can generate. A duct system that's too restrictive forces the air handler to work against high static pressure, which reduces airflow, increases energy use, and shortens equipment life. Getting this right requires real calculation, not rules of thumb.

Common Duct Design Mistakes in Florida Homes

The most common mistake is running too many duct runs through the attic when a conditioned crawlspace or interior chase would keep the ducts in the conditioned envelope. Every foot of duct inside conditioned space is a foot that doesn't lose energy to the attic heat. The second most common mistake is undersizing return air pathways. Many Florida homes have a single centrally located return grille — fine for small homes, but inadequate for larger layouts where doors are frequently closed. Bedroom doors closed against a single return can create significant pressure imbalances, causing air to leak out of the envelope through electrical outlets and gaps rather than through the return path.

Flex duct — the corrugated flexible ductwork commonly used in Florida — is also frequently installed incorrectly. Flex duct must be pulled taut with no sags or sharp bends; a sagging or kinked flex duct run can have three to five times the resistance of a properly installed run, which dramatically reduces airflow to that room. Florida code and manufacturer installation instructions both specify maximum deflection and bend radius for flex duct, but field installation often doesn't meet these standards.

Duct Leakage Testing

Florida energy code requires duct leakage testing on new construction and major HVAC system replacements. The test — typically a blower door test or a duct pressurization test — measures how much air escapes from the duct system before reaching the rooms it's intended to condition. Code requires duct leakage to be no more than 4 CFM25 per 100 square feet of conditioned floor area for ducts located outside the conditioned space. Failing this test requires sealing and retesting — a delay that costs time and money. Designing and installing the system correctly the first time is always cheaper than fixing it after a failed test.
