Whole-Home Generator Systems: MEP Design for Power Resilience in Florida

After every major Florida hurricane, the conversation turns to generators. Extended power outages — measured in days or weeks, not hours — are a real probability in much of the state, and the difference between a properly engineered standby generator system and a portable generator running on the driveway is the difference between staying home comfortably and sleeping in a hotel. A whole-home standby generator starts automatically, runs on natural gas or propane, and powers your home's essential systems without any intervention from you. But getting the system right requires real engineering, not just buying the biggest generator at the home improvement store.

Sizing a Generator: It's All About the Load Calculation

Generator sizing starts with a load calculation — the same kind used for electrical service sizing, but focused specifically on the loads you want to run during an outage. Most homeowners don't need to power everything: deciding in advance what you're willing to live without (pool heater, EV charger, electric dryer) versus what's essential (HVAC, refrigerator, well pump, medical equipment, lighting) allows for a much smaller and less expensive generator than trying to power the whole house at its maximum demand.

The critical calculation is inrush current — the spike of power that electric motors draw at startup. Your central HVAC system might draw 5,000 watts continuously but 20,000 watts for the first second when the compressor starts. The generator and its transfer switch must handle this starting load without dropping voltage or stalling. Getting the inrush calculation wrong is the most common cause of generator underperformance — the unit starts but can't pick up the HVAC load, defeating the main purpose of having it.

Automatic Transfer Switches: The Critical Link

An automatic transfer switch (ATS) is what transforms a generator from a convenience into a true resilience system. The ATS continuously monitors utility power and, when it detects an outage, signals the generator to start, waits for it to reach stable output, and then transfers the home's loads from utility power to generator power — all within 10–30 seconds, without any action from the homeowner. When utility power returns, the ATS transfers back and signals the generator to shut down after a cool-down period.

The ATS must be installed by a licensed electrician and permitted. The connection to the utility meter base must comply with the utility's requirements — most Florida utilities require a physical interlock that prevents the generator from back-feeding the grid, which would be a serious safety hazard for utility workers. The MEP engineer specifies the ATS rating, connection method, and interlock design to comply with both the NEC and the utility's interconnection standards.

Fuel: Natural Gas vs. Propane

In areas with natural gas service (available in parts of Central and North Florida, less common in South Florida), natural gas is the preferred fuel for standby generators because it doesn't require on-site storage and supply doesn't run out during extended outages. Natural gas supply pressure may need to be verified and the gas line sized for the generator's BTU demand — a 22kW generator draws approximately 285,000 BTU/hr at full load, which is a significant demand on a residential gas system already serving a furnace, water heater, and range.

In areas without natural gas — most of South Florida — propane is the standard fuel for standby generators. A 250-500 gallon propane tank provides 5–10 days of full-load operation for a mid-size generator, which covers most hurricane scenarios. The tank location must satisfy setback requirements from property lines, structures, and ignition sources, and the propane supply line must be sized for the generator's BTU demand. The MEP engineer coordinates the fuel system design with the generator manufacturer's requirements and the local authority having jurisdiction.

Permits and the Inspection Process

A standby generator installation in Florida requires both an electrical permit and a mechanical/gas permit. The electrical scope covers the ATS, the generator's electrical connection, and any critical loads panel work. The gas scope covers the propane or natural gas supply line. Both are inspected separately. The generator also typically requires a concrete pad designed by the manufacturer or a structural engineer, and in many counties, the generator's sound output must meet local noise ordinances — something worth verifying before you commit to a generator location close to a neighbor's bedroom window.
