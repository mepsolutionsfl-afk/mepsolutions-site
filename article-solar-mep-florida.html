Solar Panel Integration: What the MEP Engineering Looks Like for Florida Homes

Florida gets more sunshine than almost any other state, and rooftop solar has become an increasingly attractive investment as panel costs have dropped and utility rates have risen. But a solar installation is a significant electrical system — one that connects to both the home's wiring and the utility grid — and it requires careful engineering to be safe, code-compliant, and actually optimized for your roof and energy consumption. Understanding the MEP engineering behind a solar installation helps you ask better questions of your installer and make decisions that affect system performance for the next 25 years.

The Electrical Engineering Behind a PV System

A rooftop solar system generates DC power from the panels, which is converted to AC power by the inverter and fed into the home's electrical system. The engineering choices start with the inverter: string inverters (one inverter for the whole array), microinverters (one small inverter per panel), or power optimizers (panel-level DC optimization with a central inverter) each have different performance characteristics, shading tolerance, monitoring capabilities, and costs. A shaded roof — from trees, neighboring buildings, or the home's own dormers — benefits significantly from microinverters or power optimizers, which prevent a shaded panel from reducing output for the entire string.

The electrical plan needs to show panel layout, string configurations, conduit routing from panels to inverter, inverter location, AC disconnect location, and the interconnection point with the home's electrical panel. Florida utility interconnection requirements (NEC Article 705) dictate how the solar system connects to the existing panel and how back-feeding to the grid is metered and protected. A load calculation verifies that the existing panel has adequate capacity for the solar interconnection, or identifies whether a panel upgrade is needed.

Structural Coordination: Roof Loading and Attachment

Solar panels add dead load to the roof structure — typically 3–5 psf for the panels, racking, and hardware. Florida's wind speed requirements, among the highest in the continental US, mean that panel attachment must be engineered to resist hurricane uplift forces. The structural engineer reviews the roof framing and certifies that the existing structure can support the panel load and wind uplift, or specifies strengthening work if it can't. In Florida, structural review is typically required for permitted solar installations, and installers who skip this step create real liability for themselves and for the homeowner.

Panel placement must also account for the roof's pitch, orientation, and shading to maximize energy production. South-facing panels at Florida's latitude (25–30° N) produce the most annual energy, but west-facing panels produce more energy during afternoon peak demand hours — which may have value if your utility uses time-of-use rates. An energy model can quantify the trade-offs and help you make the right choice for your specific rate structure.

Battery Storage Integration

Pairing solar panels with battery storage is increasingly popular in Florida — particularly after Hurricane Ian and the extended outages it caused. A battery system stores excess solar production during the day and uses it to power the home at night or during a grid outage. The engineering for a battery system involves load analysis (what do you want the battery to power, for how long), battery sizing (kWh capacity), inverter compatibility (not all inverters support battery integration), and electrical design for the battery's connection to both the solar system and the home's critical loads panel.

Florida utilities have specific interconnection requirements for systems with battery storage, particularly around anti-islanding protection — the requirement that the system cannot feed power back to the grid during an outage (which would create a safety hazard for utility workers). Modern battery-integrated inverter systems handle this automatically, but the engineering must document compliance with the utility's requirements before interconnection approval is granted.

The Interconnection and Inspection Process

Installing a solar system in Florida requires a building permit, an electrical permit, and utility approval before the system can be turned on and connected to the grid. The utility interconnection application — submitted to FPL, Duke Energy, TECO, or your local municipal utility — reviews the system specifications and issues permission to operate (PTO) after the building department inspections are complete. Utility review timelines vary: FPL's residential interconnection typically takes 4–8 weeks from application to PTO. Factoring this timeline into your project schedule prevents the frustration of having a fully installed system sitting idle while waiting for utility approval.
