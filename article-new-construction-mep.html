MEP Basics October 6, 2026

What Goes Into New Construction MEP Coordination

On a new construction project, every trade has its own set of drawings — the architect draws the walls and finishes, the structural engineer designs the beams and slabs, and the MEP engineers design the mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems. In theory, they should all work together seamlessly. In practice, without deliberate coordination, you get conflicts: a supply duct that runs through a structural beam, a plumbing stack that exits where a post was supposed to go, or an electrical panel installed exactly where the HVAC closet needs to be. These conflicts are expensive to fix in the field. They're cheap to fix on paper.

What MEP Coordination Actually Means

MEP coordination is the process of reviewing all the discipline drawings together to identify and resolve conflicts before construction begins. On commercial projects, this is often done with BIM (Building Information Modeling) software that creates a 3D clash detection model. On residential projects, it's typically done by overlaying the drawings and reviewing each floor systematically — checking that ductwork fits within the ceiling plenum, that pipe routes don't conflict with structural members, and that electrical panels and equipment have adequate clearance for service access.

Good coordination also means checking that penetrations through fire-rated assemblies are properly identified, that plumbing vents can reach the roof without being blocked by trusses or structural members, and that mechanical equipment is positioned where it can actually be maintained — because a condensate pan you can't reach to clean is a water damage incident waiting to happen.

Common Conflicts That Kill Schedules

The most common coordination failures on Florida residential projects involve ductwork and trusses. Engineered roof trusses are designed for specific loads and can't be field-modified without an engineering analysis — but HVAC installers sometimes try to notch or cut them to make a duct fit. This is a structural safety issue and a code violation. Proper coordination identifies the conflict during design and resolves it by rerouting the duct or designing a chase.

Plumbing and structural conflicts are equally common, particularly around drain lines that need to drop below a slab. If the structural engineer hasn't been told that a drain needs to pass through a certain location, a grade beam or footing may end up exactly where the pipe needs to go. Resolving this in the field means saw-cutting concrete — an expensive and disruptive fix that coordination would have prevented for free.

How MEP Engineers Coordinate with the Architect

On a well-run project, the MEP engineer receives the architectural drawings early — during schematic design, not after construction documents are complete — and uses them to lay out systems that fit within the available space. This means knowing ceiling heights, wall thicknesses, mechanical room dimensions, and where structural elements are located before committing to a system layout.

In Florida, coordination also involves understanding local utility requirements. FPL, Duke Energy, and other utilities have specific requirements for electrical service entrance locations, transformer clearances, and meter placement that aren't always reflected in the architect's drawings. The MEP engineer needs to coordinate these requirements into the design so the utility connection is straightforward on the day of inspection.

Why It Matters More in Florida

Florida's climate adds a layer of complexity that makes MEP coordination especially important. Humidity management is critical — if supply air and return air pathways aren't properly coordinated, you can end up with dead zones where humid air stagnates and mold grows. Attic conditions in Florida reach 150°F in summer, so equipment located in unconditioned attic spaces needs to be rated for those conditions and accessible for service without the installer passing out from the heat.

Hurricane strapping, impact glass, and energy code requirements also interact with MEP system placement in ways that aren't always obvious. A well-coordinated set of construction documents accounts for all of it — giving every trade a clear path forward and giving the building department a permit package they can approve with confidence.
