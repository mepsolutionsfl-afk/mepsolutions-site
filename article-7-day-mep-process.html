What Actually Happens During Your 7-Day MEP Turnaround

"Permit-ready in 7 days" is the line most people notice first, and it's usually followed by a reasonable question: how can a full set of mechanical, electrical, and plumbing plans come together that fast without something getting rushed? The honest answer is that the timeline isn't the product of working faster under pressure — it's the result of a repeatable process that removes the guesswork, back-and-forth, and idle time that typically stretch a design schedule out over several weeks.

The clock starts with intake, and this step matters more than most people expect. Before any calculations begin, we collect the architectural plans, site information, and any specifics about the project — square footage, number of stories, special systems like a pool, generator, or solar array, and the jurisdiction the permit will be filed in. Florida has dozens of Authorities Having Jurisdiction, each with its own submittal quirks, and knowing which one applies before drafting starts avoids costly rework later. Missing or unclear information at this stage is the single biggest cause of delay on any project, ours included, so this front-loaded review is what makes the rest of the week possible.

Once the scope is locked, the engineering work begins in parallel rather than in sequence. HVAC load calculations, electrical service and panel sizing, and plumbing fixture unit counts are all developed at the same time by team members who are already communicating about how those systems will share space inside the walls, ceilings, and mechanical closets. Running these disciplines side by side, instead of handing a project down a line from one engineer to the next, is one of the main reasons the schedule can compress without cutting corners.

From there, the calculations get translated into drawings. This is where coordination becomes just as important as the math — a duct chase that conflicts with a structural beam, or an electrical panel located where a plumbing vent needs to run, is far cheaper to catch on screen than in the field. Our drafting process builds in cross-checks between the mechanical, electrical, and plumbing sheets specifically so these conflicts surface before a contractor ever sees the plans, not after.

Before anything goes out the door, a second set of eyes reviews the full package against the Florida Building Code and the relevant local amendments. This internal check looks for the kinds of issues that trigger plan reviewer comments and add weeks to a permit timeline: missing calculations, inconsistent labeling between sheets, or a detail that doesn't match current code language. Catching these internally, rather than letting a municipal reviewer find them, is what keeps a 7-day design turnaround from turning into a much longer permit approval process.

The final step is delivery — a stamped, submittal-ready set along with support if the reviewing jurisdiction comes back with questions. Plan reviewers occasionally ask for clarification even on a clean set, and being responsive at that stage is part of the same process that got the plans out quickly in the first place. A fast turnaround only means something if the plans hold up once they're in front of a reviewer.

None of this means every project is identical or that timelines never flex — a highly custom luxury home with complex systems takes more coordination than a straightforward addition, and we communicate that upfront rather than promising a number that doesn't fit the scope. But the underlying process — parallel engineering, built-in cross-discipline coordination, and an internal code review before submittal — is what makes a fast, permit-ready turnaround a standard we can deliver on project after project, not a one-time exception.
