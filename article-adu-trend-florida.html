The ADU Boom: What Florida's Accessory Dwelling Unit Trend Means for Your MEP Plans

Accessory dwelling units — often called ADUs, in-law suites, or backyard cottages — have become one of the most talked-about trends in Florida residential construction. Many municipalities across the state have eased zoning restrictions in recent years, making it easier for homeowners to add a detached or attached secondary living space on their property. Whether the goal is housing an aging parent, creating space for adult children, generating rental income, or simply adding flexible square footage, ADUs are showing up in more backyards than ever before.

On the surface, an ADU can look like a simple project: a small structure with a bedroom, bathroom, and kitchenette. In practice, Florida Building Code treats most ADUs as their own independent dwelling unit, which means the mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems have to be engineered almost as if you were designing a small standalone home — not just an extension of the existing house. That distinction catches a lot of homeowners and builders off guard once permitting begins.

Electrical capacity is usually the first question that comes up. Does the existing panel have enough spare capacity to feed a sub-panel in the new unit, or does the main service need to be upgraded? Will the ADU have its own meter, or share the primary residence's utility connection? Each answer changes the load calculation, the wire sizing, and ultimately what the local utility and building department will require before they sign off.

Plumbing brings its own set of questions. A new kitchen and bathroom mean new fixture counts, which affect water supply sizing and drain-waste-vent design. For homes on septic systems, adding a full dwelling unit can trigger a review of whether the existing septic capacity can support the additional occupancy, or whether an upgrade is needed. Homes on municipal sewer typically have an easier path, but tie-in location and line sizing still need to be verified early rather than assumed.

HVAC decisions for an ADU usually come down to independence versus integration. A dedicated mini-split or small packaged system gives the secondary unit its own comfort control and keeps the two living spaces thermally separate — often the preferred approach for a rental unit or an in-law suite where occupants want independent control. Tying into the primary home's ductwork can work in some layouts, but it requires a fresh load calculation to confirm the existing system can handle the added square footage without sacrificing comfort in the main house.

Because an ADU is generally permitted as a distinct structure, it also needs its own complete, code-compliant set of MEP drawings rather than a simple sketch added to the primary home's plans. Getting those drawings right the first time — sized correctly, coordinated with the existing utilities, and aligned with the Florida Building Code — is what keeps an ADU project moving through permitting instead of stalling on review comments. As this trend keeps growing across Florida's neighborhoods, homeowners and builders who plan the engineering early tend to have a much smoother path from concept to occupancy.
