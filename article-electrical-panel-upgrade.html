When to Upgrade Your Electrical Panel: A Florida Homeowner's Guide

Most Florida homes built before 2000 were wired for a lifestyle that looks nothing like today's. No EV chargers, no whole-home generators, no tankless water heaters drawing 50 amps at startup. If your home still runs on a 100-amp or 150-amp panel — or, worse, has a Federal Pacific or Zinsco panel — you're likely running out of capacity just as your power demands are peaking. Understanding when an upgrade is necessary, and what it involves, can save you from nuisance tripping, code violations, or a dangerous electrical fire.

Signs Your Panel Needs to Be Upgraded

The most obvious sign is a breaker that trips repeatedly under normal load. If you're resetting the same 20-amp circuit every time the microwave and coffee maker run at the same time, that circuit may simply be overloaded — but if it's a pattern across multiple circuits, the panel itself may be undersized. Other warning signs include a panel that's warm to the touch, breakers that won't reset, flickering lights when large appliances start, or a fuse box (rather than a breaker panel) that predates modern safety standards.

In Florida, the heat and humidity also accelerate wear on electrical components. Rust on the panel interior, visible corrosion on breaker connections, or any signs of moisture intrusion are red flags that warrant immediate attention from a licensed electrician and an engineer to assess what's safe to keep versus what needs full replacement.

How Modern Life Demands More Power

A Level 2 EV charger draws 40–50 amps continuously. A central HVAC system in a 2,500-square-foot Florida home draws 30–50 amps just for the compressor. Add a hot tub, a tankless water heater, an induction range, and a home office full of computers and monitors, and a 100-amp service is completely inadequate. Most electrical engineers today recommend a minimum 200-amp service for new Florida homes and 400-amp service for larger or all-electric homes.

Upgrading to 200 amps or 400 amps isn't just about adding breaker slots — it often means coordinating with your utility provider (FPL, Duke Energy, TECO, etc.) to upgrade the service entrance, which has its own lead time and permitting process. Starting that conversation early prevents delays.

What the Upgrade Process Involves

A panel upgrade in Florida requires a permit from your local building department and inspection by a licensed electrical inspector. The process typically involves a load calculation prepared by a licensed electrical engineer, a new panel and breakers installed by a licensed electrician, a service entrance upgrade coordinated with the utility, and a final inspection before power is restored. In some cases, the utility will also require a new meter base.

The load calculation is the starting point — it determines exactly what size service you need based on your existing and planned loads. Skipping this step and just "going bigger" can leave you paying for 400-amp service when 200 amps would have been sufficient, or the reverse: installing 200 amps and then needing to go back to the utility six months later when you add a hot tub.

Florida Permit and Inspection Requirements

Every panel upgrade in Florida requires a permit — no exceptions. Working without a permit exposes homeowners to fines and, more importantly, to liability if a fire occurs on unpermitted wiring. Florida's electrical code follows the NEC with state amendments, and local amendments vary by county. Miami-Dade, for instance, has stricter requirements around arc-fault protection and tamper-resistant receptacles than the base NEC. Your engineer of record and electrician should know the local amendments for your county.
