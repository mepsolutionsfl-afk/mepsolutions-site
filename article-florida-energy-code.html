Florida Energy Code Compliance: What Builders and Homeowners Need to Know

Florida's energy code — officially the Florida Energy Conservation Code (FECC) — is updated regularly and is consistently one of the more demanding codes in the Southeast. If you're building or doing a major renovation in Florida, energy compliance isn't optional, and it's not something you can address as an afterthought. The compliance documentation needs to be part of your permit application, and an MEP engineer typically prepares it. Understanding what the code requires helps you make smarter decisions early in the design process — before choices become expensive to change.

What the Florida Energy Code Covers

The FECC covers four main areas: the building envelope (walls, roof, windows, and doors), the mechanical system (HVAC), the water heating system, and lighting. For residential construction, the envelope and HVAC requirements are typically the most significant cost drivers. The code sets minimum efficiency ratings for HVAC equipment, maximum U-factors and solar heat gain coefficients (SHGC) for windows and glazing, and minimum insulation R-values for walls, roofs, and floors.

Florida's solar intensity is what makes the SHGC requirement particularly important. A window that's highly efficient in Minnesota because it retains heat is a liability in Miami because it also admits solar heat gain. Florida's code requires windows to have low SHGC values — typically 0.25 or lower in Climate Zone 1, which covers most of South Florida — to limit solar heat gain and reduce the cooling load on the HVAC system.

Prescriptive vs. Performance Compliance

There are two paths to demonstrating energy code compliance in Florida. The prescriptive path requires meeting specific minimum values for each component — HVAC efficiency must be at least X, windows must have SHGC no greater than Y, insulation must be at least R-Z. This path is straightforward but inflexible: if you want to use less efficient windows, you can't offset them with better insulation under the prescriptive path.

The performance path uses energy modeling software — typically REScheck or EnergyGauge — to demonstrate that the proposed building performs as well as or better than a reference building that meets the prescriptive requirements. This path allows trade-offs: a more efficient HVAC system can offset less efficient windows, for example. It requires more engineering time upfront but often results in lower overall construction costs by allowing the builder to optimize the design rather than meet every individual requirement in isolation.

HVAC Efficiency Requirements

Florida requires a minimum SEER2 rating of 15.2 for central air conditioning systems in most climate zones — a requirement that already exceeds the federal minimum and gets updated as the code is revised. For heat pumps, the minimum HSPF2 rating also applies, though in Florida the cooling efficiency matters far more than the heating efficiency given the mild winters. Duct systems must be tested for leakage, and ducts located outside the conditioned space — in an attic, for example — must be insulated to a minimum of R-8.

One often-overlooked requirement is the Manual J load calculation. Florida code requires that HVAC equipment be sized based on a load calculation, not on rules of thumb like "one ton per 500 square feet." An oversized system short-cycles, fails to dehumidify properly, and costs more to run — all serious problems in a Florida climate. The load calculation needs to be documented and submitted with the permit application.

Water Heating Compliance

Florida's energy code encourages efficient water heating, and solar water heating systems can earn significant compliance credit in the performance path. Heat pump water heaters — which are two to three times more efficient than conventional electric resistance heaters — are increasingly popular in Florida because they work well in warm climates and contribute meaningfully to overall energy performance. The code sets minimum energy factors for water heaters by type and fuel source.

For large homes with multiple bathrooms, the code also addresses hot water distribution — specifically, the pipe length between the water heater and the fixtures. Long pipe runs mean wasted water and energy while you wait for hot water to arrive. Designs that meet the code's pipe length limits or that include recirculation systems with occupancy controls satisfy this requirement.
