Case Study: Coordinating MEP for an Elevated Coastal Custom Home

A note before we start: the project below is an illustrative, composite example built from patterns we see repeatedly on Florida coastal custom homes — not a specific address or client. We're using it to walk through how MEP coordination actually plays out on a project like this, and why it matters for the timeline.

Picture a new custom home on a coastal lot, elevated on a raised foundation to meet the required base flood elevation for its flood zone. On paper, that's an architectural decision. In practice, it reshapes almost every MEP system in the house. Electrical panels, the water heater, and HVAC air handlers all have to sit above the flood elevation line, which usually means a mechanical room or utility platform gets built into the elevated structure itself — and that space has to be sized and located before duct runs, panel feeds, or plumbing risers can be finalized. Get the sequencing wrong, and you end up resizing a mechanical closet after the architectural plans are already drawn.

Electrical coordination is where this kind of project tends to get complicated fast. A coastal custom home like this often carries a full load list: a whole-home generator with an automatic transfer switch, pool and spa equipment, an EV charger circuit, and sometimes dedicated circuits for exterior lighting and dock or seawall equipment. Each of those is manageable on its own, but the panel has to be sized for all of them together, from day one, elevated and all. We've seen projects where the panel was sized for "the house" early on, then the pool and generator got added later as change orders — which is exactly the kind of mid-construction surprise a coordinated load calculation is meant to prevent.

On the mechanical and plumbing side, proximity to salt air and water changes some of the usual Florida defaults. Outdoor HVAC equipment needs wind-rated anchoring that accounts for higher exposure, condensate lines have to be routed and pitched correctly through an elevated structure instead of a simple slab run, and a tankless water heater is often the better fit given how little mechanical space is typically available under an elevated home. None of these are exotic decisions, but each one has to be made with the others in mind — a duct run that works on paper can conflict with a plumbing stack once you're actually coordinating a tight mechanical platform in three dimensions.

This is also where having architectural drafting and MEP engineering under one roof pays off. Instead of the architect finalizing the elevated floor plan and then handing it to an engineer to "make it work," our drafting and engineering teams coordinate the mechanical platform, panel location, and riser paths while the architectural plan is still being drawn. That back-and-forth is what keeps a reviewer from kicking back a plan set because the electrical plan shows a panel location the structural plan doesn't support, or the mechanical plan assumes a duct chase that was never built into the floor plan.

That coordination is also what makes a 7-business-day, permit-ready turnaround realistic once site information and architectural plans are finalized. It's not that the engineering happens faster in isolation — it's that the disciplines aren't fighting each other by the time they reach permit review. Fewer conflicting assumptions between architectural, electrical, mechanical, and plumbing means fewer rounds of plan reviewer comments, which is usually the real source of permitting delays on a project like this, not the engineering itself.

The broader lesson, even outside flood zones and coastal lots: the earlier MEP coordination happens relative to the architectural design, the fewer surprises show up during construction. If you're planning a custom home with its own site-specific constraints — elevation requirements, a tight lot, a long equipment list — that's exactly the kind of project where getting engineering and drafting talking to each other early is worth the conversation before plans are finalized.
