Tankless vs. Traditional Water Heaters: What Florida Homeowners Should Know

Water heating accounts for roughly 14–18% of a typical home's energy use, making it the second-largest energy expense after HVAC. In Florida, where hot water demand is year-round (unlike northern states where a cold shower in winter is simply not happening), the choice of water heating technology has a real impact on energy bills and on the design of the home's plumbing and electrical systems. Tankless water heaters have become popular for good reasons — but switching from a tank-style system isn't always as simple as swapping the unit.

How Tankless Heaters Work and Why They're Popular

A tankless (or "on-demand") water heater heats water only when you need it, by passing cold water through a heat exchanger activated by flow. There's no tank of pre-heated water sitting idle, which eliminates standby heat loss — the energy wasted keeping 50 gallons of water hot 24 hours a day whether you use it or not. In Florida's hot climate, where the incoming cold water temperature is already 70–75°F (compared to 40°F in northern states), the heat exchanger doesn't have to work as hard, which improves efficiency.

A properly sized tankless unit can supply hot water indefinitely — you won't run out during a long shower or when multiple fixtures are running simultaneously, which is the most common complaint about standard tank heaters in homes with multiple bathrooms. For a household that uses a lot of hot water, the endless supply can be a genuine quality-of-life improvement, not just a marketing claim.

The Electrical and Gas Infrastructure Requirements

Here's where the switch from tank to tankless gets complicated. An electric tankless water heater for a whole house draws 100–150 amps at 240V during operation — far more than the 4,500-watt (approximately 19-amp) elements in a typical 50-gallon tank heater. This often requires a service upgrade: you simply can't add 150 amps of demand to a 200-amp panel that's already loaded to 160 amps. An electrical load calculation should always precede an electric tankless water heater decision.

Gas tankless heaters are more practical for whole-house use in many Florida homes — they draw a much smaller electrical load (just for the igniter and controls) and use natural gas or propane for the heating. But they require gas supply lines sized for their very high BTU input: a whole-house gas tankless unit might draw 199,000 BTU/hr at full flow, compared to 40,000 BTU/hr for a standard tank heater. If your existing gas line wasn't sized for that demand, the unit will underperform — delivering lukewarm water when multiple fixtures run simultaneously — and your gas utility or plumber may require a gas line upgrade.

When a Point-of-Use Tankless Unit Makes Sense

One smart approach for Florida homes with long pipe runs is point-of-use electric tankless heaters at the fixtures far from the central water heater. A small, single-fixture electric tankless unit (18–36 amps) installed under the kitchen sink or in the master bath vanity delivers hot water instantly at that fixture without waiting for hot water to travel from the central heater. This approach is simpler electrically than replacing the whole-house system and solves the specific problem of long wait times at remote fixtures.

Point-of-use units still require a dedicated circuit, a permit, and a licensed electrician for installation. But they're a lower-risk upgrade that doesn't require replacing the entire central water heating system.

Heat Pump Water Heaters: The Often-Overlooked Third Option

In Florida's climate, heat pump water heaters (HPWHs) deserve serious consideration alongside tankless and tank-style options. An HPWH uses a refrigeration cycle — similar to an air conditioner — to extract heat from the surrounding air and transfer it to the water, making them two to three times more efficient than electric resistance heating. In Florida, where the air is warm year-round, HPWHs operate at peak efficiency throughout the year and can produce significant energy savings compared to any resistance heating option.

HPWHs do require adequate surrounding air space (at least 750–1,000 cubic feet), some noise consideration (they sound like a small refrigerator while running), and a floor drain or condensate line for the water they remove from the air during operation. These installation requirements need to be planned into the space — another reason MEP planning early in a project prevents expensive surprises later.
