What "Permit-Ready in 7 Days" Actually Involves: Inside Our MEP Drafting Process

A 7-day turnaround sounds like a marketing promise until you see what has to happen inside that week. It isn't about rushing drawings out the door — it's about running a disciplined, repeatable process that removes the guesswork and back-and-forth that usually stretches MEP drafting into weeks. For builders and architects working against a construction schedule, a tight and predictable timeline on mechanical, electrical, and plumbing plans can be the difference between a permit submittal that moves forward and one that sits in a queue waiting on paperwork. Here's a realistic look at what happens behind the scenes during those seven days.

The process starts with intake, and this stage quietly determines how smoothly everything else goes. We collect the architectural plans, site information, square footage, and any equipment or system preferences the homeowner or builder already has in mind, then identify the specific jurisdiction the project falls under. Florida doesn't have one uniform permitting process — counties and municipalities layer their own amendments, wind zone requirements, and flood provisions on top of the base Florida Building Code, so knowing exactly which authority will review the plans shapes decisions made later in the week. Incomplete architectural sets or missing site details at this stage are, by far, the most common reason a 7-day timeline slips, which is why an experienced team will often flag gaps on day one instead of discovering them midweek.

Once intake is complete, the engineering calculations begin. This covers HVAC load calculations, electrical service and panel sizing, circuit loads, and plumbing fixture unit counts — the math that determines equipment capacity, wire and pipe sizing, and system layout before a single line gets drafted. These calculations aren't a formality; they're what keeps a system from being oversized, undersized, or non-compliant once it's installed. Because this step depends on accurate inputs from intake, any gaps found here typically get resolved with a quick clarifying call rather than a long delay, which is part of why front-loaded communication matters so much.

With calculations locked in, drafting production is where the plans actually take shape. Electrical one-line diagrams and panel schedules, HVAC duct layouts and equipment placement, and plumbing riser diagrams and isometrics all get built out and cross-checked against the architectural floor plan. This is also where coordination between disciplines happens — making sure a duct run doesn't conflict with a plumbing stack, or that panel locations make sense with the electrical service entrance. Catching these conflicts internally, before submittal, is far cheaper than catching them after a plans examiner flags them.

Before anything goes out the door, the drawings go through an internal code compliance review — a second set of eyes checking the plans against the current Florida Building Code and the relevant local amendments for that jurisdiction. Most permit rejections aren't caused by major engineering errors; they're caused by small coordination gaps or a missed local requirement that a first pass can overlook. Building this review into the process, rather than treating it as an afterthought, is what makes a 7-day timeline realistic rather than aspirational.

The final step is delivery, paired with a short window for revisions. Architects, builders, or homeowners can review the completed set and request adjustments before it goes to the permitting authority, which is a far faster conversation to have with us than with a local building department mid-review. In practice, the projects that hit the 7-day mark most consistently are the ones where the information handed over at intake was complete from the start — accurate architectural plans, clear equipment preferences, and a known project address. When that happens, the rest of the week runs the way it's designed to — not because anything was skipped, but because nothing had to be redone.
