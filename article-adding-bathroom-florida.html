Adding a Bathroom in Florida: MEP Requirements and What to Expect

Adding a bathroom — whether it's a half-bath in a hallway, an ensuite for a new bedroom addition, or a pool bathroom — is one of the most common home improvement projects in Florida, and one of the most frequently permitted incorrectly. A bathroom isn't just a plumbing project; it involves electrical circuits, ventilation, and sometimes HVAC modifications, all of which require permits and inspection. Understanding the full scope of what a bathroom addition involves helps you budget accurately and avoid the rework that comes from discovering unexpected requirements mid-project.

Plumbing: Wet Walls, Venting, and the Sanitary System

The plumbing in a new bathroom needs to connect to the existing drain, waste, and vent (DWV) system. How complicated this is depends on where the new bathroom is located relative to the existing plumbing stack. A bathroom adjacent to an existing wet wall is relatively straightforward — the new drains tie directly into the stack. A bathroom on the opposite side of the house, or in a new addition, may require running new drain lines through slabs or crawlspaces, which adds significant cost and complexity.

Every drain fixture in Florida requires a vented trap — a trap that's connected to the vent stack to prevent siphoning of the water seal. The vent must connect to the roof in an approved manner, and running a new vent through an existing roof requires flashing and waterproofing details that the roofing contractor needs to address alongside the plumber. The plumbing plan for a new bathroom needs to show all of this: fixture locations, drain sizes, trap locations, vent routing, and connection points to the existing system.

Electrical: Circuits, GFCI, and Exhaust Fan

Florida's electrical code (following the NEC) requires a dedicated 20-amp circuit for bathroom receptacles, GFCI protection for all receptacles within 6 feet of any sink, and a mechanical exhaust fan with a minimum ventilation rate of 50 CFM for the toilet compartment. The exhaust fan must duct to the exterior — not into the attic, as is sometimes done incorrectly — and the duct run should be as short and direct as possible to maintain airflow performance.

If the new bathroom includes an electric heated towel bar, heat lamp, or heated floor (increasingly popular in Florida master bathrooms), these loads need dedicated circuits sized for their wattage. A heated floor in a master bath can draw 800–1,200 watts — enough to require its own 20-amp circuit and a load calculation check against the panel's available capacity.

HVAC: Conditioning the New Space

A new bathroom needs conditioned air supply and return air pathways, both for comfort and to control humidity. In Florida, an un-ventilated or poorly ventilated bathroom will develop mold within months — the combination of humidity, warm temperatures, and occasional condensation on tile surfaces creates ideal conditions. The HVAC engineer extends the duct system to supply conditioned air to the new bathroom and verifies that the existing system has enough capacity to handle the additional load. For a small half-bath, this is typically straightforward. For a large master bath with a shower, soaking tub, and significant glass area, the added load may warrant a system review.

Permit Package Requirements

A bathroom addition permit in Florida typically requires separate plumbing, electrical, and mechanical permits, each pulled by the respective licensed contractor. The architect or designer prepares the floor plan showing the fixture layout with clearances (Florida code requires 15 inches from the centerline of a toilet to any side wall or obstruction, 24 inches clear in front, for example). The plumbing engineer stamps the DWV and water supply drawings. The electrical engineer or contractor prepares the electrical plan. All of this gets submitted together with the building permit application for the structural work.

Inspections happen in sequence — rough-in plumbing before slab pour (if applicable), rough-in electrical before drywall, mechanical rough-in before drywall — and each must be passed before the next phase can proceed. Planning this inspection sequence into the project schedule prevents the most common bathroom addition delay: a contractor who doesn't call for rough-in inspection before closing the walls and then has to open them up again.
